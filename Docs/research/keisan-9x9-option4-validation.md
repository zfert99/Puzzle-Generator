# Widening the Calcudoku Ladder: Validating Option 4 for a 9×9 Keisan Engine

## TL;DR

- **Option 4 is worth pursuing, but only in a specific, de-risked order: build cage–line intersection (pointing/claiming) first as a cheap coverage/instrumentation win, and build *bounds-based* multi-line region-sum (or, better, pairwise multi-cage combination) as the genuine new hard tier — do NOT lead with exact region-sum.** The single most relevant primary artifact, Simon Tatham's `keen.c`, implements *no* region-sum deduction whatsoever, and billabob's KenKen solver — the only known rater that scores "Region Sum" — still lists "Region Products" as an unimplemented TODO. That is strong evidence the exact-sum technique is hard to make fire on multiplicative cages, exactly as your load-bearing caveat predicted.
- **The biggest threat to Option 4's thesis is real and confirmed by the ratings literature: cage–line intersection is rated LOW (billabob "1.5: Cage-region intersection (pointing/claiming)", below "2: Hidden pair" and "2: X-Wing") and region-sum only moderate (3.0–3.9), so these techniques may land as *Medium*, not *Expert*, failing to separate a fifth tier from T2.** Meanwhile "hardest technique required" is a documented-imperfect predictor of human difficulty (Pelánek's step-complexity + dependency findings; the HoDoKu-vs-SE debate), which means your Option 2 (count guesses/steps) was likely rejected too hastily.
- **Recommendation: treat Option 1 (four honest tiers) as the shipping default and Option 4 as an *upgrade path gated on measured firing rates*, not a foregone conclusion.** Uneven tier counts across variants are normal in commercial products and cause little player confusion; a K3-scale solver expansion is only justified if instrumentation shows the new techniques fire as the hardest required step on a non-trivial fraction of otherwise-Nishio boards. If that fraction is small, spend the effort on a cheaper fifth axis (no-op "Mystery" cages / operator restriction / density) instead.

## Key Findings

1. **No real Calcudoku/KenKen solver in evidence performs cross-cage region-sum (rule-of-45) deductions.** Tatham's `keen.c` does all arithmetic reasoning strictly per-cage via candidate-combination enumeration; its only cross-box step (Hard tier) is single-cage → line candidate elimination — i.e., exactly the cage–line intersection technique, not region arithmetic. Its two hardest tiers (Extreme, Unreasonable) have `NULL` solver functions and are reached only by generic Latin-square set/forcing deductions and recursive backtracking. This mirrors your own solver structure almost exactly (T1–T4 logical, then a Nishio/guess backstop).

2. **billabob's solver is the sole implementation of region-sum for KenKen, and it confirms the multiplicative gap.** It rates Region Sum 3.0 and grades multi-cage/multi-region variants up to 3.9 ("A sum with N unknown cages will give rating 3+(N-1)/5, capped at 3.9"), but its published TODO list explicitly contains "Region Products" as *not yet done*, plus "Check for valid assignments in region total solver" — meaning even the most advanced public Calcudoku rater currently applies region-sum only to additive contributions and has not solved the multiplicative-cage version.

3. **Cage–line intersection is structurally the only box-line analogue on a boxless grid and should fire often — but it is rated as an *easy* technique**, which is a direct threat to using it to create a hard tier.

4. **"Hardest technique required" is a decent but imperfect difficulty predictor.** Pelánek's large-scale human study ("more then 1700 Sudoku puzzles, hundreds of solvers … The correlation coefficient with human performance for our best metric is 0.95") found difficulty has two sources — complexity of individual steps AND structure of dependency among steps — and that the number of high-level strategies required predicts well. The Sudoku community consensus (SE = hardest step; HoDoKu = weighted sum of all steps) is that HoDoKu's step-sum better tracks solve *time*, while SE better tracks "do you know the trick." This validates re-examining step-counting.

5. **Technique-requirement gating craters yield unless generation is constructive.** Consistent with your own `maxFootholds` and 6×6 ~0.1% experiences. HoDoKu can generate puzzles containing a specific technique, but does so by filtered search, not pure random acceptance.

## Details

### Q1 — The sum-pinning problem for multi-line region-sum on arithmetic cages (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

**The core arithmetic.** In Killer Sudoku every cage total is a sum, so the rule of 45 generalizes cleanly: for a block of *k* rows or columns, the cells sum to *k·N(N+1)/2* (for 9×9, *k·45*); subtract the totals of all cages wholly inside the block, and the remainder pins the innie/outie cell(s). SudokuWiki (Andrew Stuart) and Sudopedia document this as "innies and outies" and its multi-region extension, including the "innie-outie difference" usable when a cage can't be split cleanly.

**Why Calcudoku breaks it.** A ×, ÷, or − cage's target is not a sum. Its *sum contribution* is only known once its candidate multiset is pinned. On 0-given 9×9 boards, many cages are multiplicative and un-pinned early — so exact region-sum will fire late or never. This is your load-bearing caveat, and the primary-source evidence strongly confirms it:

- **`keen.c` (primary source, read in full):** implements region-sum *nowhere*. All cage arithmetic is per-cage combination enumeration (`solver_clue_candidate`, `dscratch`/`iscratch` scratch arrays; SUB/DIV by direct 2-cell pairing, ADD/MUL by in-place combinatorial enumeration with a running `total`). The Hard tier's only cross-box deduction is (verbatim comment) "numbers which occur in a given row or column in all candidate layouts, and rule them out of all squares in that row or column that *aren't* part of this clue box." Extreme/Unreasonable have `NULL` keen-specific solvers and are handled by generic Latin-square set/forcing deductions + recursive backtracking. A widely respected real-world Calcudoku engine deliberately does *not* combine cage targets arithmetically.
- **billabob (primary source):** the *only* KenKen rater that scores Region Sum (3.0), Region Parity (3.0), and multi-region/multi-unknown-cage variants (3.1–3.9). Its TODO explicitly lists "Region Products" as unimplemented and "Check for valid assignments in region total solver" — i.e., its region-total machinery is still additive-only and a work in progress.

**Assessment of the four workarounds:**

- **(a) Bounds-based innies/outies (min/max possible sum).** *This is the implementable version and the right one to build if region-reasoning is pursued.* For each in-block cage, from its surviving candidate multisets compute the minimum and maximum possible sum contribution. The block's uncovered cells then have a sum window `[k·45 − Σmax, k·45 − Σmin]`. Pin a cell only when the window collapses (or narrows enough to eliminate candidates). This fires *earlier* than exact region-sum because it does not require every cage to be fully pinned — a loosely constrained ×-cage still contributes a bounded interval. Cost per block is O(cells) given precomputed per-cage min/max sums, which are a cheap byproduct of the candidate-combination tables the solver already maintains. **No source implements this for Calcudoku specifically; it is a sound inference from the Killer-Sudoku innie-outie *difference* method (which already reasons with sum ranges) adapted to bounded cage contributions. Flag as reasoned inference, not sourced practice.**
- **(b) Restrict to blocks where all contained cages are additive/already-pinned.** Correct and trivial to implement, but on 0-given boards the "all additive" condition will rarely hold for a whole block, so this fires seldom. Useful as a fast special case inside (a), not as the primary mechanism.
- **(c) Product invariant ("rule of 362880").** Every row/column of a 9×9 Latin square has product 9! = 362880. A multiplicative innie/outie is *mathematically* valid: divide 362880 by the product of cages wholly inside the block to pin the poking cell's product. In practice it is far weaker than the sum version: division must be exact (integer), a single un-pinned ×/÷ cage leaves a large quotient interval, and factoring 6-digit numbers is not human-reproducible at speed. No solver implements it, and billabob lists "Region Products" as still-unbuilt. **Treat as low-value; possibly useful only as a factor filter (see Q3), not a standalone tier.**
- **(d) Modular / parity variants.** A 9×9 line sums to 45 (odd), giving a usable parity constraint: the number of odd digits in any complete line is fixed. Multiple KenKen strategy guides describe parity reasoning (addition-target parity, product-cage even/odd, subtraction-target parity — e.g. kenkenpuzzle.online and reflectionsinacrackedglass's "Kenken masters" guide). billabob rates "3: Region Parity" and "3.1: Multi-region Parity" (alongside "3.1: Multi-region Sum"), so parity region-reasoning *is* implemented and rated by at least one engine. Parity is cheaper than full sum arithmetic and robust to un-pinned cages (you only need the parity of each cage's contribution, often known before the exact value). **This is a genuinely promising, under-appreciated middle technique.**

**Bottom line for Q1:** exact region-sum is the wrong thing to build first — the primary sources show it is hard, rarely implemented, and blocked on multiplicative cages. The *bounds-based* version (a) plus *region-parity* (d) are the implementable, earlier-firing forms and should be the targets if region-reasoning is pursued at all.

### Q2 — Cage–region intersection (pointing/claiming) on a boxless Latin square

**Algorithmic spec.**

- **(i) Cage wholly inside one line (claiming).** If all cells of a cage lie in a single row (or column), the cage's digits are claimed into that line: any digit present in every surviving combination of the cage can be eliminated from the other cells of that line; and the line's remaining cells cannot use digits the cage monopolizes.
- **(ii) Digit confined within a cage to one line (pointing).** For a cage spanning multiple lines, if a digit's possible positions *within that cage* all fall in one row/column, that digit can be eliminated from the rest of that row/column outside the cage.
- **(iii) Reverse claiming.** If within a line a digit can only appear in cells belonging to one cage, that cage must supply the digit, constraining the cage's combinations.

Compute all three cheaply from the per-cage candidate-combination table the solver already builds: for each cage, for each digit, track the set of cells that can hold it across surviving combinations; intersect with line membership. `keen.c`'s Hard tier is exactly deduction (ii)/(i) — so this technique is *proven implementable and useful in a real KenKen solver.*

**Firing rate on 0-given 9×9.** On a boxless grid, cages are the only non-line constraint, so cage–line intersection is the *sole* structural analogue of Sudoku's box-line reduction — it should fire frequently. No published firing-rate instrumentation for boxless 9×9 was found; the strongest available evidence is that `keen.c` relies on it as its top named tier and billabob implements it as a standard step. **Firing rate is therefore expected-high by structural argument but unmeasured — this is the single most important thing for you to instrument.**

**Two tiers?** There is a meaningful basic-vs-hard split: (1) *basic* single-cage → line elimination (keen's Hard tier), versus (2) a *multi-cage* claiming where two or more cages jointly confine a digit or set within a line — analogous to SudokuWiki's "Cage Comparison," which detects when one cage's combinations would starve another mutually-seen cage of options ("all the cells in both cages must be mutually 'seen'"). The multi-cage version is strictly harder to spot and rarer, and could plausibly anchor a higher tier.

**Difficulty rating — the serious problem.** billabob rates "1.5: Cage-region intersection (pointing/claiming)" — *below* hidden pair (2.0) and X-Wing (2.0). (Note: the version of the page fetched during this research showed a finer split, "1.2: Cage-region intersection (pointing) / 1.5: Cage-region intersection (claiming)"; either way the rating is LOW.) If your ladder inherits similar ordering, basic cage–line intersection lands as an *Easy/Medium* technique and will **not** create an Expert tier. This directly threatens Option 4's thesis for this technique: it will fire often and cheaply, improving the solver's reach (reducing the ~23% Nishio-dependent fraction), but it will *not* by itself justify a fifth named tier. Its real value is as a **coverage/correctness improvement and instrumentation baseline**, not as the Expert separator.

### Q3 — Calcudoku-native techniques with no Sudoku analogue

- **Multi-cage combination elimination.** billabob's highest-rated real technique: "4: Combination of 2 cages / 4.2: Combination of 3 cages / 4.4: Combination of 4 cages. Combination of N cages will give rating 4+(N-2)/5 with no maximum limit." Implemented by jointly enumerating combinations across pairs, then triples, then N-tuples of cages and eliminating pairings that contradict. billabob is explicit that this is "effectively bruteforcing larger and larger subdivisions of the puzzle" — powerful but shades into search, and the developer expects future named techniques to subsume it. **A *pairwise-only* bounded version (enumerate compatible combinations for adjacent/mutually-seen cage pairs, eliminate incompatible ones) is well-defined, bounded in cost, and is a strong candidate for the Expert tier** — genuinely Calcudoku-native, clearly harder than cage–line intersection, and stopping short of full Nishio. This is arguably a better "new hard technique" than region-sum.
- **Divisibility / prime-factor propagation.** A product cage's target factorization restricts combinations (e.g., a 3-cell `105×` cage in 9×9 must be {3,5,7}; a 2-cell `7×` must be {1,7}). Best folded into the combination-generation tables as a filter rather than exposed as a tier — consistent with your own ranking. Multiple strategy pages describe factoring product targets as standard practice.
- **Parity mapping across the grid.** As in Q1(d): sum-cage parity + the fixed odd/even count per line; reflectionsinacrackedglass's "Kenken masters" guide describes cross-line parity reasoning for larger grids explicitly. Implementable and native.
- **Strategy-source scan.** calcudoku.org (Patrick Min) states all its puzzles are logic-solvable without guessing and emphasizes single-combination cages and combination restriction; its forum has "Strategies" and "Degree of difficulty" threads but no published named-technique ladder. clarity-media, puzzle-magazine, conceptis, and Puzzle Genius all reduce to: single-combination cages first, then standard Sudoku techniques, then combination cross-restriction. No source describes a Calcudoku-native technique *harder* than multi-cage combination elimination that isn't just search. The academic Latin-square-completion literature (CP/SAT/IP; "How Simple Algorithms Can Solve Latin Square Completion-Type Puzzles") treats KenKen as a CSP and offers no human-reproducible deduction beyond constraint propagation.
- **AIC / chains.** billabob implements KenKen-specific AIC (rated 4.0–4.9) using bivalue cells, bilocal candidates, grouped bilocal candidates within 2 cages, and cage-combination groupings by common weak inference, plus an "Equivalence" concept it says is niche in Sudoku but *highly useful in Calcudoku* (e.g., a 2-cell `2÷` cage: "if it contains a 3, it must also contain a 6, and vice versa … these premises are 'equivalent'"). This confirms your assessment that strong links are sparse but exist and that chains fire only occasionally — lowest priority, but the Calcudoku-specific equivalence links are more available than a naive Sudoku-port would suggest.

### Q4 — Does technique tier track perceived difficulty? (monotonicity)

**The assumption is *approximately* true but has a documented failure mode.** Pelánek's "Difficulty Rating of Sudoku Puzzles: An Overview and Evaluation" (arXiv:1403.7373; 1700+ puzzles, hundreds of solvers; best-metric correlation 0.95 with human performance) found difficulty has **two** sources: "complexity of individual steps (logic operations) and structure of dependency among steps." His related finding that "the number of high-level strategies required to solve a problem without brute-force is a good measure of Sudoku difficulty" supports hardest-technique tiering — but the two-source result means step *count* and *dependency* matter independently.

**The failure mode is real and directly relevant.** The Sudoku community (enjoysudoku.com forum; SudokuPulse) is explicit: SE rates by the single hardest step, HoDoKu by the weighted sum of all steps, and **HoDoKu is better at predicting solve time** precisely because a puzzle with one hard-but-obvious "spotlight" step can be *faster* than a puzzle requiring dozens of tedious easy steps. SudokuPulse: "A puzzle where there's exactly one hidden single to find … at each step will feel tedious, even though the technique required is the same"; and "a technique that is conceptually simple but tedious to search for … may be harder in practice than a technique that is conceptually complex but easy to spot." The enjoysudoku forum documents HoDoKu anomalies where a *higher* total score gets a *lower* level label, because level = hardest step while score = summed work.

**Implication for the fork.** This is the strongest argument that **Option 2 was rejected too hastily.** A board needing many depth-1 Nishio guesses genuinely plays harder (more total work, more dependency) than one needing a single guess, even if the "hardest technique" is identical. Your worry that step-count "may just be noise" is legitimate for *raw* step totals (dominated by trivial singles), but Pelánek's evidence says the *count of hard steps* is signal. **Recommendation: instrument Nishio-step count anyway; it is cheap and may rescue a fifth tier without any solver expansion (a hybrid of Option 2 and Option 4).**

### Q5 — Generation yield under technique-requirement gating

**Expect severe yield collapse under naive rejection sampling**, consistent with your `maxFootholds` and 6×6 ~0.1% results. If the generator must produce boards whose *hardest required step* is a specific new technique, the acceptance rate is the product of (uniquely solvable) × (T1–T2 don't already crack it) × (the new technique fires) × (nothing harder than it is required). On 0-given 9×9 the middle two conditions are already rare individually.

**Constructive generation is the known mitigation.** HoDoKu explicitly supports creating puzzles that contain a specific technique (batch mode `/s /sc` step designators; documented in its Chapter 4 manual — "create sudokus, that contain specific techniques (for example to practise new ways of solving)"), and Sudoku tutorial generators plant a target pattern then build outward. For Calcudoku the constructive approach is: start from the solved Latin square, *carve cages so that the target technique's pattern is present* (e.g., arrange a block of 2–3 lines whose contained cages are mostly additive/pinnable to make bounds-region-sum fire, or place two mutually-seen cages whose combinations force a multi-cage claiming), then verify uniqueness and grade. **No published Calcudoku-specific constructive generator targeting a named technique was found — this is a build, not a copy. Flag as inference from Sudoku practice.** Practical consequence: Option 4's generation cost is dominated not by the solver expansion but by the constructive generator needed to hit acceptable yield; budget for it explicitly.

### Q6 — Sanity-check the fork

**Is 5-tier parity worth a K3-scale expansion?** Largely no, on the evidence. Commercial puzzle products routinely ship *uneven* tier counts per variant: classic Sudoku commonly spans Easy→Evil/Extreme (five or six levels) while Killer/variant modes ship three or four ("Difficulty: Intermediate to Expert"), and no source surfaced player complaints about a variant having fewer tiers than classic. Difficulty labels are widely acknowledged as arbitrary and app-specific (sudoku.coach; SudokuPulse). **Player confusion from "Keisan has 4 tiers, Classic has 5" is minimal; the trust damage from a *dishonest* fifth tier (an "Extreme" that is really just "Expert plus noise") is worse.** This favors Option 1 as the honest default.

**Other axes for a fifth tier that need no solver expansion** (evaluate each):

- **Operator restriction / no-op "Mystery" cages.** calcudoku.org's "no-op" (Mystery Calcudoku) hides the operator, forcing the solver to deduce operation *and* digits — a large, honest difficulty jump with *zero* solver-technique expansion (the grader already enumerates combinations; you just widen each cage's combination set across all operators). calcudoku.org treats no-op as among its hardest, and notes its own hardest-ever puzzle (a 9×9, 2013-04-02) was solved by only 9.6% of regular players. **Highest-value alternative fifth axis: honest, cheap, genuinely harder, and Calcudoku-native.**
- **Cage density / size distribution.** Fewer, larger cages (more multiplicative, larger combination spaces) raise difficulty and solve time; cheap to vary, but grading must confirm it doesn't just crater yield.
- **Grid size within the variant** (e.g., reserve an 11×11/12×12 for Extreme — calcudoku.org runs up to 15×15). Honest and trivial, but changes the play footprint.
- **No-pencil-marks / time pressure.** UX-layer difficulty, honest but orthogonal to logical difficulty; fine as a mode toggle, weak as a "tier."

## Verdict on Option 4 vs Option 1 vs Option 2

**Adopt a staged hybrid, not a pure choice.**

1. **Ship Option 1 now.** Four honest tiers (Easy/Medium/Hard/Expert = "needs a depth-1 Nishio guess") is correct, trivial, and defensible. Do not block release on the fifth tier.
2. **Pursue Option 4 as a gated upgrade, but re-rank the techniques.** Your expected-value ranking put region-sum first; the evidence inverts part of that:
   - **Build first (cheap, high firing rate, coverage win): cage–line intersection (pointing/claiming).** Proven in `keen.c`, structurally the only box-line analogue on a boxless grid. *But treat it as Medium, not Expert* — it will reduce the Nishio-dependent 23% and give you instrumentation, not a new hard tier.
   - **Best candidate for the actual Expert-separator: pairwise multi-cage combination elimination**, not region-sum. It is Calcudoku-native, clearly harder than cage–line intersection, bounded in cost, and billabob rates the multi-cage family highest (4.0+).
   - **Build region-sum only in bounds-based form (Q1a), plus region-parity (Q1d), and only if the above don't separate a tier.** Exact region-sum is contraindicated by every primary source (keen.c omits it; billabob's "Region Products" is unbuilt).
3. **Simultaneously instrument Option 2.** Measuring Nishio-step *count* is nearly free and the difficulty literature says it is signal. If step-count cleanly separates a heavy-guess population, you may get a fifth honest tier with no solver expansion at all — the cheapest possible win.
4. **If none of the above cleanly separates a fifth tier, take the no-op / Mystery-cage axis for "Extreme"** — honest, cheap, and genuinely harder.

**The decisive question is empirical and you are one instrumentation pass away from answering it:** on the 0-given corpus, of the ~23% of boards that currently require Nishio, what fraction become solvable with (a) cage–line intersection added, and separately (b) pairwise multi-cage combination added? If (b) converts a meaningful fraction *and* those boards feel harder than T2 boards but easier than residual-Nishio boards, Option 4 is validated. If not, ship Option 1 + a non-solver fifth axis.

**Criterion 3 — Yield.** *Prediction: naive rejection-sampling yield for "hardest step = new technique" is low (single-digit percent or worse), consistent with prior `maxFootholds`/6×6 results; constructive generation is required.* **Confidence: high that naive gating collapses yield; medium that constructive generation recovers it.** *To confirm:* measure accept rate with a simple "hardest-required = X" filter before investing in constructive generation; if <1–2%, build the constructive generator.

**Criterion 4 — Which technique.** *Prediction: cage–line intersection is the best *coverage* technique but too easy to anchor Expert; pairwise multi-cage combination elimination is the best *Expert-separator*; bounds-based region-sum and region-parity are secondary; exact region-sum and the product invariant are contraindicated.* **Confidence: medium-high on the ordering, grounded in keen.c (per-cage only), billabob (multi-cage rated highest, region-products unbuilt), and the boxless-structure argument.** *To confirm:* the isolation runs in Criterion 1 will directly rank the techniques by (firing rate as hardest step) × (separation from T2 and from Nishio).

## Staged implementation plan

**Slice 0 — Instrumentation (do first).** Add per-technique "was this the hardest required step?" logging to the existing grader. Re-run the 0-given corpus. Also log Nishio-step *count* per board. **Gate to proceed:** you now have baseline distributions and can measure every subsequent slice's marginal effect.

**Slice 1 — Cage–line intersection (pointing/claiming).** Implement (i)/(ii)/(iii) from the per-cage combination tables (mirror keen.c's Hard tier). **Gate:** measure the drop in the Nishio-dependent fraction (currently ~23%) and how often it is the hardest required step. *Expected:* large coverage gain, but classified Medium. If it fires as hardest-required on a meaningful slice AND those boards test harder than T2, consider it a soft Expert candidate; otherwise keep it Medium.

**Slice 2 — Pairwise multi-cage combination elimination.** Enumerate compatible combinations for adjacent/mutually-seen cage pairs; eliminate incompatible pairings (bounded, pairwise only — do NOT go to N-tuples, which is search). **Gate:** does it convert a non-trivial fraction of residual-Nishio boards, and does it sit between T2 and Nishio in measured/estimated difficulty? If yes → this is your Expert-separator; Option 4 is validated. If no → stop expanding the solver.

**Slice 3 — Region-parity + bounds-based region-sum (only if Slice 2 under-delivers).** Add region-parity (cheap, robust to un-pinned cages) first; then bounds-based min/max region-sum on 2–3-line blocks. **Gate:** marginal firing rate as hardest-required step above a threshold you set from Slice 0 baselines (suggest: fires as hardest step on ≥3–5% of otherwise-Nishio boards). Do not implement exact or product region-sum.

**Slice 4 — Generation.** If any Slice 1–3 technique becomes the Expert/Extreme anchor, add constructive generation that plants the technique's pattern before carving remaining cages. **Gate:** accept rate high enough to sustain the daily/large-catalog volume the product needs.

**Fallback branch (parallel, low-cost).** If Slices 2–3 fail their gates, implement the **no-op / Mystery-cage** difficulty axis for the fifth tier and/or promote **Nishio-step-count banding** (Option 2) as the Extreme separator. Either restores 5-tier parity honestly without a K3 solver expansion.
